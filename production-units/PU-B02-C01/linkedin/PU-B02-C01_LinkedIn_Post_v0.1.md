# LinkedIn publication post

I have completed a new SRAI lesson on Supervised Learning Foundations.

The central question is simple but demanding: what must be true before a prediction becomes credible?

The lesson follows the full evidence chain—from the decision and observational unit to prediction time, feature admissibility, train-validation-test design, baselines, uncertainty, subgroup assessment and accountable use.

The executed service-demand notebook provides a useful contrast. A valid chronological evaluation gives regression test RMSE 30.876, improving on a seasonal-naive RMSE of 40.496. A deliberately leaked feature produces RMSE 2.048. That smaller number is rejected because the feature would not exist at prediction time.

This is the lesson I want learners and practitioners to retain: the strongest result is not necessarily the smallest error. It is the estimate produced by the most credible operating experiment.

The production unit includes the controlled lesson, executed notebook, SRAI presentation with audience-facing narration, student assessment, instructor solutions and an executive brief.
